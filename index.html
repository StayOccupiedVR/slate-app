Why the record matters

Every prediction here is committed to a public repository before first pitch, graded against final scores and the closing line, and never edited. Wins and losses both stay on the board. That's the whole pitch: not that the model beats the market — most days it won't — but that you can verify exactly how it does.

MLB

How the model works

Team strength comes from Pythagorean win percentage — expected record from runs scored and allowed — regressed toward .500 and combined with log5. Home field adds about four points. Starters are graded against the league's actual ERA, blending season with last five starts and regressing small samples toward the mean.

The board shows lineups, scratches, weather, and bullpen availability so you can check them before betting — but the win probability itself is built only from team strength and starters. It doesn't yet model parks, defense, or lineups. When it disagrees sharply with the market, assume the model is wrong. A perfect forecaster scores about .673 on log loss; this one is at .683. Most baseball games are close to coin flips.

The two prices

What these numbers mean

A sportsbook's two prices always add up to more than 100% — the extra is its cut. Remove the cut and you get the break-even: how often your side must win before the bet pays. If your estimate is above that line, the average result is profit; the verdict shows how much per $100.

This doesn't find winners. It checks arithmetic on an estimate you supply. If the estimate is a hunch, the answer is a hunch with a dollar sign on it.

Window

What this tab does

Run backtest replays every game in your window using only what was knowable that morning — records, run differentials, and starter stats as of that date, never anything that happened after. It generates a probability for each game, then grades those probabilities against what actually happened.

How to read the scores. Brier is the average squared error — always guessing 50% scores .2500, so anything lower means the model knew something. Log loss is similar but punishes confident mistakes much harder: calling 80% on a loser hurts far more than calling 55%. For scale, a coin flip scores .693 and a perfect forecaster about .673 — baseball is mostly coin flips, so the entire range of possible skill is narrow.

Calibration is the number to trust first. It groups games by what the model said — all the ~60% calls together — and checks whether that group actually won about 60% of the time. When it does, the percentages mean what they say. When it doesn't, they're decoration, no matter how the other scores look.

Fit weights asks whether different dial settings — more weight on team strength, less on the starter, and so on — would have predicted this window better. To keep it honest it tunes on the earlier 70% of your window and grades itself only on the later 30% it never saw. Trust only the test column. The train column always looks better; that's memorization, not skill.

Two honest limits. Small windows lie — a few hundred games on a coin-flip sport means a fitted weight that "improves" things by .001 is almost certainly noise; want months, not weeks. And this tab tests the app's built-in formula. The machine-learning number shown on game cards is a separate model with its own, stricter backtest — three seasons, tested on a year it never trained on — and a live, public record graded against sportsbook closing lines. This tab is the interactive demo of the idea; the ML model is the one keeping score for real.
